## TXV valve in Clearwater - Poulin Cooling A/C and Refrigeration

A TXV valve, or Thermostatic Expansion Valve, is a refrigerant metering device that controls the amount of liquid refrigerant entering the evaporator coil by responding to suction-line temperature and pressure in real time. The TXV valve is a type of expansion valve, sitting in the broader category of refrigerant flow-control devices that regulate how much refrigerant the evaporator receives at any given moment. A TXV valve is typically serviced or replaced by a licensed contractor, such as a FL Certified Air Conditioning Contractor (License #CAC1819909), as part of air conditioning repair work.

### Parts that make up a TXV valve

A TXV valve is built from four main components. The sensing bulb clamps to the suction line and reads the temperature of refrigerant leaving the evaporator. A capillary tube carries the pressure signal from the sensing bulb to the diaphragm inside the valve body. The diaphragm flexes in response to that pressure, opening or closing the valve port to let more or less refrigerant through. Together, these four parts, sensing bulb, capillary tube, diaphragm, and valve body, form a closed feedback loop that no fixed-orifice device can replicate.

### Measurable attributes of a TX valve

A TX valve carries several measurable attributes that determine whether it matches a given system. Flow capacity is rated in tons and must align with the system's cooling load. A valve sized too small starves the evaporator, while one sized too large floods it. Operating pressure range is the span of suction and discharge pressures the valve handles without losing control; most residential valves operate across a range of roughly 50 to 450 PSI depending on refrigerant type. Refrigerant type compatibility is stamped on the valve body and must match the system charge. A valve calibrated for R-22 cannot be swapped into an R-410A system without replacement. Connection size, measured in inches, governs how the valve threads or flares into the liquid line and suction line. Sensing bulb size, in inches, affects how quickly the bulb responds to temperature changes at the suction line. Matching all five attributes to the system is the first step in any [system diagnostics](https://poulincoolingacandrefrigeration.mintlify.app/system-diagnostics) evaluation for a refrigerant-side complaint.

### How a TEV fits into the broader AC system

A TEV regulates refrigerant flow between the liquid line and the evaporator coil, placing it at the boundary between the high-pressure and low-pressure sides of the refrigerant circuit. The valve's outlet feeds directly into the evaporator, where refrigerant absorbs heat from indoor air. Its inlet connects to the liquid line coming from the condenser and metering point. Because the TEV responds to actual suction superheat rather than a fixed restriction, it keeps the evaporator operating at full capacity across a wide range of load conditions. Something a capillary tube or fixed orifice device cannot do. A capillary tube passes a fixed amount of refrigerant regardless of load, which is why systems with variable occupancy or wide temperature swings benefit from a thermostatic expansion valve instead. Proper TEV sizing and calibration directly affect the efficiency of [central air conditioning installation](https://poulincoolingacandrefrigeration.mintlify.app/central-air-conditioning-installation) projects, where the valve is selected during equipment commissioning to match the coil and compressor.

### Common signs of a faulty TXV valve

A faulty TXV valve produces recognizable symptoms that separate it from other refrigerant-side failures. The most common sign is high superheat at the evaporator outlet, meaning the refrigerant has absorbed too much heat before leaving the coil. The valve is restricting flow too much. The opposite failure, a stuck-open valve, floods the evaporator with liquid refrigerant and sends liquid slugs toward the compressor, which shows up as low superheat and frost on the suction line near the compressor. Ice buildup on the evaporator coil, short cycling, and inconsistent cooling across zones are all associated with a valve that has lost its ability to modulate. A sensing bulb that has lost its charge, the pressurized gas inside it, causes the valve to close fully, stopping refrigerant flow and making the system appear to have lost its charge entirely. Diagnosing a faulty TXV valve requires measuring suction superheat and comparing it against the manufacturer's target, typically 8 to 12 degrees Fahrenheit of superheat at the evaporator outlet.

### TXV valve adjustment and replacement

TXV valve adjustment is possible on valves with an external adjustment stem, where turning the stem changes the spring tension and shifts the superheat setpoint up or down. Most field adjustments move the setpoint in increments of 1 to 2 degrees Fahrenheit per turn, and the correct target superheat depends on the refrigerant type and coil design. Adjustment is appropriate when the valve is mechanically sound but the superheat reading is slightly off; a valve with a failed diaphragm, a discharged sensing bulb or internal corrosion needs full TXV valve replacement instead. Replacement requires recovering the refrigerant charge, brazing or flaring the new valve into the liquid line, re-attaching the sensing bulb to the suction line with the correct clamp and insulation, and recharging the system to the manufacturer's specification. New construction projects specify the valve during the design phase so it matches the coil from day one. ### Where TXV valves apply

TXV valves appear across a wide range of systems. They are used in split-system residential air conditioners, commercial rooftop units, heat pumps, refrigeration cases, and chiller systems. Any application where refrigerant load varies and precise superheat control matters. In residential split systems, the valve mounts at the indoor air handler, upstream of the evaporator coil. Commercial refrigeration cases use TEVs sized for the specific case temperature and refrigerant, with flow capacities ranging from fractions of a ton to several tons on large walk-in systems. Heat pumps use a bi-directional thermostatic expansion valve or a pair of check-valve-equipped TEVs so the system can meter refrigerant correctly in both heating and cooling modes. Refrigerant compatibility is critical. In commercial refrigeration, R-404A, R-448A, and R-449A systems each require valves calibrated to their specific pressure-temperature curves.
