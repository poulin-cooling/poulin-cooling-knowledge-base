Refrigerant recharge, called an AC recharge or freon recharge, is the process of restoring an air conditioning system's refrigerant to the manufacturer-specified charge level after a confirmed leak has been repaired. It is a type of HVAC repair procedure, not a standalone maintenance task. A refrigerant refill without first finding and sealing the leak is a temporary fix. The charge will drop again, and the underlying damage continues.

What refrigerant recharge is and what it is not

Refrigerant recharge is the controlled addition of refrigerant to bring a system back to the charge level specified on the unit's nameplate. That specification is set by the manufacturer in pounds, matched to the system's tonnage. Refrigerant does not deplete through normal use the way engine oil does. A low charge means a leak exists. Recharging without repairing that leak first is not a solution. It is refrigerant loss deferred.

An undercharged system cannot transfer heat efficiently. The evaporator coil runs too cold, ice forms on the coil, airflow drops, and the compressor works harder than it should. Left unaddressed, that strain shortens compressor life significantly. Compressor replacement is one of the most involved repairs on any AC system, so catching a low charge early matters.

Measurable attributes of a refrigerant recharge

Refrigerant recharge has three attributes that a technician measures and records during every proper service call.

  • Charge amount: Charge amount is measured in pounds and varies by system tonnage. A 3-ton residential unit typically holds between 6 and 12 pounds of refrigerant, depending on line-set length and the refrigerant type specified by the manufacturer.
  • Charging method: Charging method is either subcooling or superheat, selected based on whether the system uses a thermostatic expansion valve (TXV) or a fixed orifice metering device. Subcooling targets the liquid line leaving the condenser; superheat targets the suction line leaving the evaporator. Using the wrong method produces an inaccurate charge even if the weight added looks correct.
  • Pre-charge leak-test result: Pre-charge leak-test result is the documented confirmation that the leak has been located and repaired before refrigerant is added. A passing result is required before recharge begins. Refrigerant handling requires EPA Section 608-certified technicians. That certification governs who may legally purchase and handle refrigerants under federal law.

How refrigerant recharge relates to the broader AC system

Refrigerant recharge sits inside the larger category of AC repair and is almost always performed as one step within a multi-step service visit, not as a standalone appointment. The sequence matters. A technician first identifies the leak source. Common locations include the evaporator coil, the Schrader valves, the line-set connections, and the condenser coil. The leak is repaired, the system is pressure-tested, and only then is the refrigerant refill performed.

The refrigerant type connects this procedure to the broader regulatory environment. R-22 (Freon) was phased out of production in 2020 under EPA regulations, making it expensive and increasingly scarce. Most systems installed after 2010 use R-410A, and newer equipment uses R-454B or R-32 under updated efficiency standards. A technician working with any of these refrigerants must hold EPA Section 608 certification. Signs a system needs an AC refrigerant recharge

Signs a system needs a refrigerant recharge appear before the system fails completely. Recognizing them early keeps a minor refrigerant loss from becoming a compressor replacement.

  • Warm air from the vents: Warm air from the vents at normal thermostat settings is the most direct sign that the system cannot absorb enough heat from indoor air.
  • Ice on the evaporator coil or refrigerant lines: Ice on the evaporator coil forms when the coil temperature drops below freezing due to insufficient refrigerant pressure, restricting airflow and compounding the problem.
  • Longer run cycles: Longer run cycles occur because the system cannot reach the set temperature efficiently, so the compressor runs continuously trying to compensate.
  • Hissing or bubbling sounds: Hissing or bubbling sounds near the indoor or outdoor unit often indicate refrigerant escaping through a crack or a failed valve.
  • Higher energy use: Higher energy use without a change in usage patterns points to a system working harder than it should, a common result of refrigerant loss.

How often AC recharge is needed and where it applies

Recharge frequency depends entirely on whether the system has a leak. A properly sealed system with no leak should never need a refrigerant refill over its entire service life. A system requiring recharging every one to two seasons has an active leak that has not been fully repaired. Repeated recharges without leak repair accelerate compressor wear. They increase refrigerant costs over time.

Refrigerant recharge applies to central split systems, package units, mini-split systems, and commercial rooftop units. The charging method and refrigerant type differ across these platforms. The core procedure, however, remains consistent: verify the leak, repair it, pressure-test, then recharge to nameplate specification.
