Pressure testing, called a System Pressure Check, is a leak detection process that introduces pressurized gas into a sealed refrigerant system and monitors the system for pressure loss, identifying the location and severity of leaks before refrigerant is charged or a system is returned to normal operation. As a type of leak detection, it sits at the foundation of refrigerant system diagnostics. A failed pressure test stops a bad installation or a corroded line set from becoming a refrigerant loss event. Scope and component parts of pressure testing

Pressure testing covers the full sealed circuit of a refrigerant system. This includes the line set, evaporator coil, condenser coil, and all fittings, valves, and brazed joints. The core components required to run the test are a pressure gauge, a valve adapter to connect the gas supply to the system ports, and in some cases a sealant or isolation valve to section off a specific portion of the circuit. Nitrogen is the industry standard. It is dry, inert, and will not contaminate the system the way refrigerant or compressed air can, which makes it the preferred choice for new installations and post-repair checks. A technician connects the nitrogen supply through the valve adapter, brings the system to the target test pressure, closes the supply, and watches the gauge over the hold period.

Measurable attributes: test pressure, hold time and pressure decay rate

Measurable attributes of pressure testing include four values that determine whether a system passes or fails. Test pressure is the target pressure applied to the system, typically 150 to 600 psi depending on the refrigerant type and system design, with high-pressure refrigerants such as R-410A requiring higher test pressures than older low-pressure refrigerants. Hold time is the duration the system must maintain that pressure without supply. Field checks commonly use 15 to 30 minutes. Thorough commissioning tests on new installations may extend to several hours. Pressure decay rate is the rate at which pressure drops during the hold period, measured in psi per minute; a system with no leaks shows zero decay, while even a small leak produces a measurable drop. Longer hold times improve sensitivity. A digital manifold gauge can resolve pressure changes that an analog gauge misses entirely. AC system load calculation helps determine appropriate pressures for the specific equipment. TXV valve installation requires this confirmation step on the circuit.

How pressure testing relates to the broader refrigerant system

Pressure testing is a process performed on a refrigerant system, and its result directly determines what happens next in the installation or repair sequence. A passing test clears the system for evacuation and refrigerant charge. A failing test sends the technician back to locate and repair the leak before any refrigerant enters the circuit. Requirements can vary and should be confirmed with the EPA. Pressure testing versus vacuum testing and dye testing

Pressure testing and vacuum testing are not the same process. Each serves a distinct purpose in the commissioning sequence. A pressure test finds leaks by applying positive pressure and watching for decay, while a vacuum test, run after the pressure test passes, removes moisture and non-condensables by pulling the system below atmospheric pressure. Dye testing is a separate leak detection method that introduces a fluorescent dye into the refrigerant circuit and uses a UV light to locate the dye at the leak point; it is useful for finding slow leaks in a system already in operation, but it cannot substitute for a pre-charge pressure test on a new or repaired system. Hydrostatic testing uses water or liquid instead of gas and applies in some industrial piping contexts, but it is not used on refrigerant circuits because moisture contamination damages the system. The formal name for the hold-and-watch method is the Pressure Decay Test. It is the version most commonly referenced in equipment manufacturer commissioning guides.

Common signs that pressure testing is needed

Pressure testing is indicated whenever a refrigerant system shows signs of refrigerant loss, has undergone any repair to the sealed circuit, or is being commissioned for the first time. A system that short-cycles, loses cooling capacity over a season, or trips on a low-pressure fault is showing signs consistent with a refrigerant leak that a System Pressure Check can confirm or rule out. Ice forming on the suction line or evaporator coil is another indicator, because low refrigerant charge causes the coil to drop below freezing even when the outdoor temperature is high. After any repair involving the line set, coil, or compressor, refrigerant pressure testing confirms the repair is sealed before the system is recharged. A licensed air conditioning contractor performs the test with calibrated gauges and records the start pressure, hold duration and end pressure as documentation of the result. Where pressure testing applies across system types

Pressure testing applies to split systems, packaged units, ductless mini-splits, commercial refrigeration circuits, and any other sealed refrigerant system regardless of size. Every application follows the same logic. Requirements can vary and should be confirmed with the local building authority. Commercial refrigeration systems run continuously and carry larger refrigerant charges; they are tested at commissioning and after any compressor or coil replacement. Test pressure and hold time for a commercial walk-in cooler circuit differ from those for a residential split system because operating pressures and refrigerant types differ. The underlying process is identical in every case: apply dry nitrogen to the target pressure, isolate the supply, hold for the required duration, and read the gauge. Split system air conditioner circuits receive the same procedure as ductless mini-split installation line sets.
