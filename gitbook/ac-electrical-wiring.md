### Component parts of AC electrical wiring

AC electrical wiring consists of six primary components. Together they deliver stable power to the cooling system. Conductors serve as the current-carrying cores, typically copper or aluminum, and are sized to the load drawn by the unit. Each conductor is surrounded by an insulation jacket rated for the temperature and moisture conditions present at the installation site. Termination lugs connect conductors to breaker terminals and equipment lugs while avoiding resistance at the joint. Connectors join wire segments inside junction boxes and keep the circuit mechanically secure. Circuit breakers protect the wiring from overcurrent by interrupting the circuit when current exceeds the rated ampacity. These parts together create the complete AC circuitry that allows a cooling system to operate safely.

- **Conductors:** Conductors carry current from the panel to the unit, sized in American Wire Gauge (AWG) to match the ampacity of the circuit.

- **Insulation jacket:** Insulation jacket protects conductors from heat, moisture and abrasion, with THHN/THWN being the standard type for HVAC applications.

- **Termination lugs:** Termination lugs secure conductors at breaker terminals and equipment connection points to minimize resistance at the joint.

- **Connectors:** Connectors join wire runs inside junction boxes and maintain a continuous, mechanically stable circuit path.

- **Circuit breakers:** Circuit breakers interrupt the circuit when current exceeds the rated ampacity, protecting conductors from heat damage.

### Measurable attributes of AC electrical wiring

AC electrical wiring carries several measurable attributes that determine whether a circuit is correctly sized for the equipment it serves. Voltage matters. Residential and light-commercial AC systems run at 208 to 240 volts, the standard line-voltage class that separates this wiring from low-voltage thermostat circuits. Current capacity ranges from 15 to 30 amperes depending on the unit's rated load, and the wire gauge must match that ampacity. Wire gauge is expressed in AWG: a lower AWG number means a thicker conductor with higher current capacity, so a 30-ampere circuit typically calls for 10 AWG copper. Conductor material affects both resistance and long-term reliability; copper has lower resistivity than aluminum and is less prone to oxidation at connection points. Insulation type must be rated for the installation environment, and THHN/THWN insulation is the standard choice because it handles both dry and wet conduit runs. Requirements can vary and should be confirmed with the local building authority for the wiring to be safe and effective.

### How AC electrical wiring relates to the broader AC system

AC electrical wiring connects directly to the AC unit as one component within the larger AC system between the electrical panel and the mechanical equipment. Without properly rated wiring the compressor, condenser fan motor and air handler lack stable voltage, so voltage fluctuations at the equipment terminals produce premature motor failure and compressor damage. AC wiring remains separate from the refrigerant system that moves heat through phase changes in refrigerant and from plumbing pipes that handle condensate drainage. It remains separate from low-voltage control wiring and thermostat wiring that carry 24-volt signals between the thermostat, control board and contactor rather than line-voltage power to the motors. A licensed [air conditioning contractor](air-conditioning-contractor.md) typically evaluates both the line-voltage and low-voltage circuits together because a fault in either layer can prevent the system from operating. ### Common problems and signs of faulty AC electrical wiring

AC electrical wiring develops faults through heat cycling, moisture intrusion, pest damage, and loose terminations that work free over years of vibration. Some signs are unmistakable. A tripped breaker that resets but trips again within a short time is one of the clearest indicators that the wiring or a connected component is drawing more current than the circuit is rated for. Burn marks or discoloration at the disconnect block or contactor terminals indicate a high-resistance connection generating heat at the joint. Flickering or dimming lights in the home when the AC starts point to a voltage drop on the circuit, which can mean undersized conductors or a failing connection. Melted insulation on conductors inside the air handler or disconnect is a direct sign that the wiring has been carrying more current than its rating allows. Electrical wiring inspections during routine AC maintenance typically help catch these conditions before they cause equipment damage or create a fire hazard. During maintenance, electrical wiring inspections typically involve checking conductor insulation condition, torquing termination lugs to manufacturer specifications, and verifying that the breaker rating matches the equipment nameplate, because an oversized breaker will not trip at the current level that damages the wiring. ### Where AC electrical wiring applies

AC electrical wiring applies in every setting where a vapor-compression cooling system is installed: single-family homes, multi-family buildings, light commercial spaces, and dedicated equipment rooms. No installation is exempt. Residential split systems typically draw 15 to 30 amperes at 240 volts, while larger commercial packaged units may require higher-ampacity circuits sized to the specific equipment. In multi-unit residential buildings, each system receives its own dedicated circuit so that a fault on one unit does not affect the others. Rooftop packaged units in commercial applications use the same wiring principles but often require longer conduit runs from the electrical room to the rooftop, which increases the importance of correct conductor sizing to keep voltage drop within acceptable limits. Anywhere a cooling system operates, the AC circuitry behind it must be matched to the equipment's electrical specifications to keep the system running reliably and safely.
