## Dedicated circuit in Clearwater - Poulin Cooling A/C and Refrigeration

A dedicated circuit, called a dedicated branch circuit, is a type of electrical circuit reserved exclusively for one high-draw appliance or system. No other device shares its wiring, breaker, or load capacity. Central air conditioning units, electric dryers, refrigerators, and electric ranges are among the appliances that require one. Unlike a general-purpose circuit that feeds multiple outlets across a room, a dedicated line carries its full rated amperage to a single load without competition from other devices on the same breaker.

### Measurable attributes: amperage, voltage and single-load designation

Dedicated circuits carry specific, measurable ratings that must match the appliance they serve. The three attributes that matter most are amperage, voltage and single-load designation.

- **Amperage rating:** Amperage rating for a dedicated branch circuit ranges from 15A for small dedicated loads up to 60A for large central air conditioning systems. Most residential central AC units draw between 15A and 60A depending on tonnage, so the breaker and wire gauge must be sized to the nameplate data on the unit, not to a generic standard.

- **Voltage rating:** Voltage on a dedicated electrical circuit runs at either 120V for smaller appliances or 240V for central air conditioners, heat pumps and electric dryers. A central AC unit almost always requires a 240V dedicated line, fed by a double-pole breaker in the service panel.

- **Single-load designation:** Single-load designation means exactly one device connects to the circuit. A dedicated circuit for an AC unit cannot feed a garage door opener, a workshop outlet or any other load, even intermittently. That exclusivity is what makes the circuit "dedicated" in the first place.

Wire gauge follows amperage directly. A 20A circuit uses 12-gauge copper; a 30A circuit uses 10-gauge; a 60A circuit steps up to 6-gauge or larger. ### How a dedicated circuit relates to the central air conditioning system

A dedicated circuit is a required component of every central air conditioning installation, sitting between the main service panel and the condensing unit or air handler. The AC unit depends on it. Stable, uninterrupted power at the correct voltage and amperage is what the circuit provides. A shared circuit introduces voltage sag whenever another appliance starts, which stresses the compressor motor and shortens its service life. The relationship is direct: the dedicated line powers the AC unit, and the AC unit cannot operate safely or reliably without it.

### Dedicated circuit vs. Shared circuit: the key contrast

A dedicated circuit stands in direct contrast to a shared circuit, called a general-purpose circuit or multi-outlet general circuit. Shared circuits distribute load. A general-purpose circuit feeds multiple outlets and devices simultaneously, spreading its rated amperage across whatever happens to be plugged in. Putting a central AC unit on a shared circuit means the compressor competes for current with lights, televisions, and small appliances every time it starts. That competition causes nuisance tripping, voltage fluctuations, and, over time, compressor damage. A dedicated electrical circuit eliminates that competition entirely by reserving the full breaker capacity for the single appliance it serves.

### Common problems and signs of a faulty or missing dedicated circuit

Dedicated circuit problems fall into a recognizable pattern that shows up during system diagnostics when a technician investigates repeated breaker trips or unexplained compressor failures.

- **Nuisance tripping:** Nuisance tripping happens when the AC unit shares a breaker with other loads and the combined draw exceeds the breaker's rating, causing it to open on startup even though the unit itself is not faulty.

- **Voltage sag:** Voltage sag occurs when a high-draw appliance on a shared circuit pulls the line voltage below the AC unit's operating tolerance, typically below 10% of rated voltage, stressing the compressor on every start cycle.

- **Overheated wiring:** Overheated wiring results from undersized wire gauge carrying more current than it is rated for, a situation that develops when a dedicated line is wired with the wrong gauge for the breaker size.

- **Breaker failure:** Breaker failure follows repeated overloads. A breaker that trips frequently weakens internally and eventually fails to open under fault conditions, removing the protection the circuit was designed to provide.

- **Compressor burnout:** Compressor burnout is the downstream consequence of sustained voltage sag or repeated hard starts caused by an inadequate or shared circuit, and it is one of the most expensive AC failures a homeowner faces.
